# Guard training clips: script drafts (round 1)

Status: first draft, written from general security practice, not yet from
company procedure. Nothing here should be produced as video until the owner
has corrected it.

How to read this:

- Each clip teaches one skill and runs about 90 seconds (roughly 200 spoken words).
- **Narration** is what the voice says. **On screen** is what the guard sees.
- **[CONFIRM: ...]** marks a place where the script guesses at your rule.
  Replace each one with what your company actually requires.

---

## Clip 1: Walking a patrol

**After watching, a guard can:** walk a patrol that actually finds problems,
and knows what to do with what they find.

| # | On screen | Narration |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 1 | A guard walking a corridor on autopilot, phone in hand. A propped-open door passes behind them, unnoticed. | A patrol is not a walk. If you finish your round and can't say what changed since the last one, you weren't patrolling. |
| 2 | Title: "Know what normal looks like." Split view of the same loading dock: tidy, then with one pallet moved and a light out. | Your first rounds on a site are for learning what normal looks like. Which doors are locked. Which lights are on. Which cars belong. After that, your job is to notice what is different. |
| 3 | Checklist appearing item by item: doors and locks, lights, alarms and panels, water and smells, people and vehicles. | On every round, check the same things. Doors and locks. Lights. Alarm panels. Leaks, smoke, or strange smells. And anyone, or any vehicle, that doesn't fit the time and place. |
| 4 | Two route maps of the same building, drawn in different orders, with different clock times. | Don't be predictable. Change your route and your timing, so nobody watching can count on where you'll be. Still hit every checkpoint. [CONFIRM: are checkpoints scanned or logged, and how often is a round required?] |
| 5 | Guard stops at the propped door, steps back, speaks into a radio. | When something is wrong, stop before you touch it. Is it safe to go closer? If you're not sure, it isn't. Keep your distance and call it in. [CONFIRM: who they call first: supervisor, control room, or client contact] |
| 6 | Guard writing a note with the time visible. End card: "Notice. Check. Report. Write it down." | Then write it down, with the time, even if it turned out to be nothing. A round with no notes looks the same as a round that never happened. |

## Clip 2: Writing an incident report

**After watching, a guard can:** write a report that someone who wasn't there
can rely on.

| # | On screen | Narration |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 1 | A report that reads "Guy was acting weird near the back. Told him to leave." A manager reads it and frowns. | Your report will be read by people who weren't there: your supervisor, the client, sometimes police or an insurer. If it's vague, it's useless to them, and it doesn't protect you. |
| 2 | Six words appear in turn: Who, What, When, Where, How, What you did. | Every report answers six things. Who was involved. What happened. When. Where exactly. How you came to know about it. And what you did. |
| 3 | Two columns. "Opinion": "He was drunk and aggressive." "Fact": "He was slurring, smelled of alcohol, and shouted 'I'll be back' twice." | Write what you saw and heard, not what you concluded. Don't write "he was drunk." Write what you observed: slurred speech, smell of alcohol, unsteady on his feet. Let the reader draw the conclusion. |
| 4 | A timeline: 22:14 noticed, 22:16 spoke to him, 22:19 he left by the north gate, 22:21 supervisor informed. | Put it in order, with times. Use people's exact words when they matter, in quotation marks. Describe people so a stranger could pick them out: height, build, clothing, anything distinctive. |
| 5 | Guard writing at a desk; a wall clock shows only a few minutes after the incident. | Write it as soon as the situation is safe, while it's fresh. [CONFIRM: deadline for the report, for example before end of shift, and where it is filed] |
| 6 | The opening bad report is replaced by a clear rewritten version. End card: "Facts. In order. With times." | If you make a mistake, correct it openly. Never change a report to make it look better. A report that is plain and honest is the one that holds up. |

## Clip 3: Handling a confrontation

**After watching, a guard can:** bring the temperature down, and knows when
to stop talking and step back.

| # | On screen | Narration |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 1 | An angry man at an entrance, voice raised, a guard facing him. Freeze frame. | Someone is angry and in front of you. Your goal in the next two minutes is not to win. It is that everyone, including you, goes home unhurt. |
| 2 | Overhead diagram: guard standing at an angle, two arm-lengths away, a clear path behind them marked in green. | Start with where you stand. Keep about two arm-lengths of distance. Stand slightly side-on, hands open and visible. Make sure you have a way out behind you, and don't block theirs. |
| 3 | Two speech bubbles. Crossed out: "Calm down!" Kept: "I can hear this is frustrating. Tell me what happened." | Lower your voice and slow down. People match the tone they hear. Don't tell anyone to calm down; it never works. Let them talk, and show you heard it: "I can see this is frustrating. Tell me what happened." |
| 4 | Speech bubble: "I can't let you in through here, but I can call the manager, or you can use the front desk." | Then give choices instead of orders. Say what you can't do, and offer what you can. A person with a choice has a way to back down without losing face. |
| 5 | Warning signs appear around the man: clenched fists, stepping closer, a weapon outline, a second person joining. The guard steps back and raises a radio. | Know when talking is over. If they move into your space, make threats, or you see a weapon, step back, get distance, and call for help. [CONFIRM: who they call, and at what point they call police] |
| 6 | Text: "Property can be replaced." | You are not there to fight. [CONFIRM: your policy on physical contact, restraint, and detaining people. This depends on local law and your client contracts and must be stated exactly.] |
| 7 | Guard writing a report afterwards. End card: "Distance. Listen. Choices. Step back." | Afterwards, report it, even if it ended quietly. The next guard on that post needs to know. |

## Clip 4: The people side of the job

**After watching, a guard can:** be the approachable, professional face of the
site without giving away what they're there to protect.

| # | On screen | Narration |
|---|-----------|-----------|
| 1 | Morning at a lobby. Staff walk past a guard who looks up, nods, and greets them. | Most of your shift is not incidents. It's people: staff, visitors, deliveries, the public. For many of them, you are the first person they meet on this site. |
| 2 | Side by side: a slouched guard on a phone, and the same guard upright, uniform neat, eyes up. | People decide in seconds whether you're someone to respect. Uniform in order, phone away, eyes up. Looking alert also puts off anyone who came to cause trouble. |
| 3 | A lost visitor being pointed toward a reception desk. | Greet people first. A simple "Good morning, can I help you?" is good service, and it's good security too. Someone with no good reason to be there does not like being noticed. |
| 4 | A regular employee without a badge, smiling: "Come on, you know me." The guard smiles back and gestures to the sign-in desk. | Be friendly, and stay consistent. The rule applies to the person you like and the person in a hurry. "I know it's a pain. I still need to see your pass." [CONFIRM: your access and sign-in rules] |
| 5 | Icons with a line through them: site details, shift times, colleagues' details, social media. | Be careful what you share. Don't discuss the site, its routines, your colleagues, or incidents with anyone who doesn't need to know. Not in conversation and not online. |
| 6 | Guard handing over to the next shift, pointing at the log. End card: "Friendly. Fair. Discreet." | And look after your team. Show up on time, hand over properly, and tell the next guard what they need to know. |

## Questions the drafts raise

1. Where do your guards work (country, and state or province)? This decides
   what Clip 3 may say about physical contact and detaining people.
2. Who does a guard call first when something is wrong, and when do they call
   police directly?
3. How are patrol checkpoints recorded, and how often is a round required?
4. Where are incident reports filed, and by when?
5. Which language or languages should the clips be in?
